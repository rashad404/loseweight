Your TDEE, or total daily energy expenditure, is how many calories your body uses in a day. For most adults it lands somewhere between 1,600 and 3,000. Every calorie target you'll ever set comes from this number, so it's worth knowing what goes into it and how much to trust the estimate.

The four parts

Resting metabolic rate accounts for 60 to 70 percent of the total. It's what you'd burn lying still all day: heart, brain, kidneys, liver, and tissue maintenance. It scales mostly with lean body mass, which is why bigger and more muscular people have higher resting rates.

Digestion takes about 10 percent, and the cost varies by what you eat. Roughly 20 to 30 percent of protein calories go to processing the protein itself, versus 5 to 10 percent for carbohydrate and close to nothing for fat. This is part of why higher protein diets slightly raise expenditure.

Everyday movement, sometimes called NEAT, is 15 percent or more and varies enormously between people. Walking, standing, chores, posture, fidgeting. Differences of several hundred calories a day between two similar people are common, and this component drops when you're in a deficit.

Deliberate exercise is usually only 5 to 10 percent, which surprises people. A hard 45 minute session might cost 300 to 400 calories, less than the day-to-day variation in everyday movement.

Estimating it

Mifflin-St Jeor is the standard for adults:

Men: (10 x kg) + (6.25 x cm) - (5 x age) + 5
Women: (10 x kg) + (6.25 x cm) - (5 x age) - 161

If you know your body fat percentage from a DEXA scan, Katch-McArdle is usually more accurate because it works from lean mass directly: 370 + (21.6 x lean body mass in kg). Body fat readings from a bathroom scale aren't reliable enough to be worth substituting in.

Then multiply by activity:

FactorDescribes
1.2Desk job, little deliberate movement
1.375Light exercise 1 to 3 days a week
1.55Moderate exercise 3 to 5 days a week
1.725Hard exercise 6 to 7 days a week
1.9Physical job or training twice a day

If you train four times a week but otherwise sit at a desk, 1.375 to 1.55 is closer than 1.725. Overshooting here is the most common way people end up with a target that's too high.

How much to trust it

Predictive equations land within 10 percent of measured expenditure for about two thirds of people. For the other third the error is larger, and you can't tell in advance which group you're in without indirect calorimetry, which most people won't do.

That's not a reason to skip the calculation. It's a reason to treat it as a starting hypothesis and correct it against three weeks of your own scale data.
