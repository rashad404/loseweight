If your weight has been flat for three weeks or more while you've genuinely kept doing what you were doing, that's a plateau. Anything shorter is normal fluctuation. And the most common cause isn't metabolic damage, it's that your deficit shrank while you weren't looking.

First, check whether it's real

Body weight swings 1 to 2 kg day to day from water, sodium, glycogen, gut contents, and where you are in your menstrual cycle. Those swings are bigger than a week's worth of fat loss. A genuine 0.5 kg per week works out to about 70 g a day, which a single reading can't detect.

So the test is: no downward movement in your 7 day average across three or more consecutive weeks. Two flat weeks means nothing at all.

Why progress actually stalls

Your deficit got smaller on its own

This is the main one. Someone who has lost 10 kg needs roughly 100 to 200 fewer calories a day than they did at the start. If you're still eating what you ate then, the 500 calorie deficit you set up might now be 300. Still real, still working, just slow enough to disappear into the daily noise.

Portions crept up

Reported food intake drifts upward over months, and you can't see it from the inside. Studies using doubly labeled water, which measures energy expenditure directly, have repeatedly found people underreport intake by 20 to 40 percent, with the gap widening the longer a diet runs. This isn't dishonesty. Serving sizes just quietly expand.

Adaptive thermogenesis

Energy expenditure does fall a bit more than body composition change alone predicts. The effect is real, and it's usually in the range of tens to low hundreds of calories a day. It gets described as "starvation mode," which overstates it considerably. It slows you down. It doesn't stop you.

You're moving less

In a deficit, spontaneous activity tends to drop: fewer stairs, less fidgeting, more sitting. This one can quietly account for several hundred calories a day, and it's entirely unconscious.

You're building muscle

If you recently started resistance training, fat loss and muscle gain can cancel out on the scale. Your waist measurement and how your clothes fit will show what the scale is hiding.

What to change, in what order

  1. Confirm it with data. Weigh daily, read only the 7 day average, and make sure the flat stretch really covers 21 days or more.
  2. Weigh your food for one week instead of estimating. This alone resolves a large share of plateaus, and it's less work than it sounds for seven days.
  3. Recalculate your targets at your current weight rather than your starting weight. This is the direct fix for the most common cause.
  4. Add movement before you subtract food. Steps are easier to sustain than a tighter food budget, and they protect the spontaneous activity that tends to fall.
  5. Only then cut 100 to 150 calories, and wait two weeks before judging it.
  6. Consider two to four weeks at maintenance. It doesn't undo what you've lost, and it often restores the consistency that was actually the problem.

What tends to backfire

Slashing calories hard increases lean tissue loss and rarely survives a normal week. Adding hours of cardio raises appetite and cuts into recovery, and burns fewer calories than most people assume. Cutting out food groups or doing a cleanse doesn't address any mechanism that causes a plateau, so there's nothing for it to fix.

Most plateaus are arithmetic, not metabolism. Recalculate at your current weight, verify what you're actually eating, and add movement before cutting further.
