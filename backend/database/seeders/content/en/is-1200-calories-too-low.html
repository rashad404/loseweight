For most adults, yes. 1,200 calories is a floor, not a target, and it's far too low for anyone whose maintenance is above about 1,600. A better rule is to eat 15 to 25 percent below what you actually burn, whatever number that produces.

Where 1,200 came from

It's a long-standing convention for the lowest intake at which a varied diet can still plausibly meet an adult woman's vitamin and mineral needs. It was a safety floor. Somewhere along the way it became a default target in diet plans and apps, which is a different thing entirely.

Do the comparison yourself

Take a 30 year old woman, 170 cm, 75 kg, lightly active. Her total daily expenditure is around 2,100 calories. At 1,200 she's running a 43 percent deficit, well outside the 15 to 25 percent range that preserves lean mass and keeps a plan livable.

An oversized deficit costs you in ways that don't show on the scale. More of the weight lost comes from muscle. Hunger becomes constant, concentration suffers, and training quality drops. Micronutrient shortfalls become likely, particularly iron, calcium, and B vitamins. In women, sustained low energy availability can disrupt the menstrual cycle. And adherence collapses, which is the one that actually determines where you are in a year.

Sensible floors

Women: generally not below 1,200 calories without clinical supervision. Men: generally not below 1,500.

But the percentage rule is the more useful one, because it scales with the person. A 25 percent deficit means something different for someone burning 3,000 calories than for someone burning 1,800. A fixed number doesn't adapt at all.

When very low calorie diets do make sense

Very low calorie diets, typically around 800 calories a day using formula meal replacements, have a genuine evidence base. Trials of type 2 diabetes remission have used them with meaningful results.

The difference is everything around them: medical supervision, micronutrient supplementation, monitoring, and a structured plan for reintroducing food. That's a clinical intervention with a defined endpoint. It isn't the same as setting an app to 1,200 and hoping.

What to do instead

Work out your expenditure at your current weight, subtract 15 to 25 percent, and check the result against the floors above. If the arithmetic pushes you below the floor, raise your activity rather than cutting food further. Then set protein at 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg, which matters more the tighter your calorie budget gets.

If you have a history of disordered eating, calorie targets can do real harm regardless of the number. Talk to a clinician before starting any restriction.
