If you're losing weight, aim for 1.6 to 2.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. If your BMI is above 30, scale to your goal weight instead and use 1.2 to 1.5 g per kilogram. For a 75 kg person that's roughly 120 to 165 g a day.

Protein is the one macronutrient with consistent, well-replicated evidence behind it during weight loss, and the reason is specific: it changes how much of what you lose is fat rather than muscle.

What it actually does in a deficit

When you're eating below maintenance, your body draws on both fat and lean tissue. Adequate protein combined with resistance training shifts that ratio meaningfully toward fat. Without either, a larger share of the weight you lose is muscle, and muscle is both harder to regain and part of what keeps your metabolic rate up.

Protein is also the most satiating macronutrient. At matched calories, higher protein meals reliably reduce reported hunger, which matters more than it sounds when the hard part of dieting is compliance rather than knowledge.

There's a smaller third effect: 20 to 30 percent of protein calories are spent digesting and absorbing it, versus almost none for fat. Real, but modest. Don't build a plan around it.

Choosing your number

SituationTarget
Weight loss, BMI under 301.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight
Weight loss, BMI 30 and above1.2 to 1.5 g per kg of goal weight
Age 65 and over, in a deficit1.2 to 1.5 g/kg, toward the higher end
Maintenance, sedentary0.8 to 1.2 g/kg

The goal weight adjustment matters at higher BMIs. A 130 kg person doesn't need 260 g of protein a day, and trying to eat it would crowd out everything else. Scaling to a target of around 85 kg gives a workable 100 to 130 g.

You may have seen 0.8 g/kg quoted as the recommended amount. That figure is a floor for preventing deficiency in sedentary people. It was never meant as an optimum for someone losing weight.

Spreading it out

Muscle protein synthesis responds to per-meal doses of roughly 25 to 40 g. Three or four meals in that range work better than one large serving at dinner, which is how most people naturally eat.

For reference: 150 g of chicken breast has about 46 g, 200 g of Greek yogurt about 20 g, a cup of cooked lentils about 18 g, two eggs about 12 g, 150 g of firm tofu about 17 g, and a scoop of whey about 24 g.

If you eat mostly plants, aim toward the higher end of the range and vary your sources. Individual plant proteins are somewhat less digestible and have less complete amino acid profiles, so the effective intake is lower than the label total.

Is high protein safe

In people with healthy kidneys, intakes in the 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg range have not been shown to cause kidney damage. This has been looked at repeatedly.

Existing kidney disease is a genuine exception, not a disclaimer. If you have reduced kidney function, your protein intake has to be set by the clinician managing it, and the right number may be well below anything on this page.

One practical downside worth mentioning: hitting these targets usually costs more money and more cooking than eating the same calories in carbohydrate and fat. That's a real constraint, and it's worth planning around rather than discovering in week three.
