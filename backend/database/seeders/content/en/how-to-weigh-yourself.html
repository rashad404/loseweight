Weigh yourself every morning under the same conditions, record every reading including the bad ones, and judge progress only from the 7 day rolling average. A single day's number tells you almost nothing, because daily fluctuation is roughly ten times larger than daily fat loss.

The arithmetic behind that

A genuine 0.5 kg per week of fat loss works out to about 70 g a day. Normal day to day swings run 1 to 2 kg. So on any given morning, the noise is between fifteen and thirty times the signal. No single reading can detect what you're looking for.

An average over seven readings can, because the fluctuations largely cancel out while the trend doesn't.

Where the swings come from

A salty meal retains water for a day or two. Each gram of stored glycogen holds around 3 g of water, so a high carbohydrate day adds visible weight that isn't fat. Food still in transit weighs what it weighs. Water retention commonly peaks in the late luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, often 0.5 to 2 kg. A hard training session draws water into muscle for repair, so a good workout can show up as a gain the next morning. Poor sleep and stress both promote fluid retention.

None of these are failures. They're just water moving around.

How to do it

  1. Weigh daily rather than weekly. More readings mean a cleaner average, and daily weighing is associated with better outcomes in weight management trials.
  2. Same conditions each time: morning, after using the bathroom, before eating or drinking, minimal clothing.
  3. Same scale, on a hard floor. Carpet throws off the reading.
  4. Record every number, including the ones you don't like. Deleting bad days corrupts the average, which defeats the point of averaging.
  5. Compare this week's average to last week's. Never compare two single days.

Reading the trend

Once you average, the picture usually resolves into a stepwise descent: flat or rising for a week, then a drop. This pattern is normal. Fat cells appear to retain water transiently as they empty, releasing it in a batch, which is why loss often arrives in visible steps rather than smoothly.

Judge your rate over three weeks minimum. Two flat weeks is not a plateau. See the guide on plateaus for what actually counts as one.

When daily weighing is the wrong tool

For some people, particularly anyone with a history of disordered eating, daily weighing raises anxiety and drives restriction. That cost outweighs whatever the data is worth.

Weekly measurements, waist circumference, progress photos, or simply how a fixed pair of trousers fits are all legitimate substitutes. If the number on the scale is setting your mood for the day, put the scale away and talk to a clinician about it. That's a reasonable thing to raise, and it comes up often.

Track more than weight

Weight alone hides body composition change, which matters if you've started resistance training. Add waist circumference monthly, how your clothes fit, what you can lift, and your resting heart rate. When the scale stalls, these frequently keep moving, and they're often the better evidence that what you're doing is working.
