Aim for about 14 g of fiber per 1,000 calories you eat. On a 1,600 calorie intake that's roughly 22 g a day. Most people in Western countries get 15 to 18 g total, which is about half of what's recommended.

Fiber does something protein can't: it makes a smaller amount of food feel like enough, mostly by taking up space and slowing how fast your stomach empties.

The per-calorie framing matters

You'll usually see fiber targets given as flat numbers, typically 25 g a day for women and 38 g for men. Those assume you're eating at maintenance. If you're in a deficit at 1,600 calories, chasing 38 g is uncomfortable and not especially useful. Scale it to what you're actually eating.

Why it helps

High fiber foods are bulky and water-rich, so they stretch the stomach and trigger fullness signals at fewer calories. Viscous soluble fiber slows gastric emptying, which blunts the blood sugar rise after a meal and extends how long you feel fed. Gut bacteria ferment fiber into short chain fatty acids that influence appetite-regulating hormones, though how much this contributes in practice is still being worked out.

There's also a simpler reason. Foods high in fiber are almost always also high in micronutrients and low in energy density, so eating more of them displaces things that aren't.

In trials where the only dietary instruction was to eat 30 g of fiber a day, with no calorie target at all, participants lost meaningful weight. Not as much as with a structured calorie prescription, but from a single, easy-to-remember rule.

Soluble and insoluble

Soluble fiber, in oats, beans, apples, and psyllium, forms a gel, slows digestion, and lowers LDL cholesterol. Insoluble fiber, in wheat bran, vegetable skins, and nuts, adds bulk and speeds transit. You want both, and whole foods give you both without any need to track which is which.

Increasing intake without misery

Going from 15 g to 35 g overnight produces gas and bloating, and this is the point where most people give up. Increase by about 5 g a week and drink more water as you do. Fiber without enough fluid makes constipation worse rather than better, which is the opposite of what people expect.

Some easy additions: a cup of cooked lentils has about 15 g, a medium avocado about 10 g, two tablespoons of chia seeds about 10 g, a medium pear with skin about 6 g, half a cup of raspberries about 4 g.

Supplements

Whole foods are better because the fiber arrives with everything else that's in the food. But psyllium husk is well studied and reasonable as a gap-filler if you're consistently short, at 1 to 2 teaspoons in water before a meal. It won't replace vegetables, and it isn't meant to.
