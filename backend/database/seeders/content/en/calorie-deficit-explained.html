A calorie deficit means eating less energy than your body spends. It's the mechanism behind fat loss: without one, stored fat doesn't come off, whatever else changes. To calculate yours, estimate what you burn in a day and subtract 15 to 25 percent. For most adults that works out to 300 to 750 calories a day.

The harder question is what to expect from that deficit, because the rule most people are handed gives an answer that's too optimistic.

Estimating what you burn

Start with your resting metabolic rate, the energy you'd use lying still all day. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the best validated of the simple formulas:

Men: (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age) + 5
Women: (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age) - 161

Then multiply by an activity factor to get your total daily expenditure: 1.2 if you're sedentary, 1.375 for light exercise a few days a week, 1.55 for moderate, 1.725 for hard training most days, 1.9 if you train twice daily or do physical labor. Most people pick a number here that's one step too high.

Subtract 15 to 25 percent of that total and you have your target intake.

Why the 3,500 calorie rule overpromises

You've probably seen the rule that 3,500 calories equals a pound of fat, so a 500 calorie daily deficit means a pound a week. The energy content is roughly right. The problem is what it assumes about you.

It treats your daily expenditure as fixed. It isn't. As you lose weight you get lighter, so every step and every heartbeat costs less. You carry slightly less lean tissue, which lowers your resting rate. You also move less without deciding to: fidgeting, stair-taking, and general restlessness all decline in a deficit. On top of that, energy expenditure drops a little further than body composition alone predicts, an effect called adaptive thermogenesis.

Applied literally, the 3,500 calorie rule says a 500 calorie daily deficit produces over 50 pounds of loss in a year and keeps going indefinitely. Dynamic models built from metabolic ward data, including the NIH Body Weight Planner, predict roughly half that in year one, with weight flattening toward a new plateau. That gap is why so many people conclude they're broken when they're actually on track.

How big to make the deficit

Bigger deficits produce faster early loss. They also cost more lean tissue, generate more hunger, and get abandoned more often. Adherence, not deficit size, is what predicts where you land at twelve months.

A rate of 0.25 to 0.5 kg (about 0.5 to 1 lb) per week works well for many people and preserves muscle better than faster loss. If you have a lot to lose, up to 1 percent of body weight per week is reasonable. Below roughly 1,500 calories a day for men or 1,200 for women, it gets difficult to meet vitamin and mineral needs from food, so that's the point where supervision matters.

There's a practical argument for the smaller deficit too. A 300 calorie gap absorbs a bad week. A 900 calorie gap doesn't, and one bad week that turns into three is worse than three months of slower progress.

Treat the number as a hypothesis

Predictive equations carry real error at the individual level. Your actual expenditure might sit 10 to 15 percent either side of the estimate, and there's no way to know which without measuring. So use the calculated figure as week one's experiment. Track your weight for three weeks, compare the trend against what the calculation predicted, and adjust by 100 to 200 calories in whichever direction the data points.

That correction step matters more than getting the initial estimate exactly right.
