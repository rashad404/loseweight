This page is general education. It doesn't recommend any medication for you, because that decision depends on your full medical history and belongs with a clinician who has it.

Where medication fits

Obesity treatment is usually described in tiers that stack rather than replace each other. Diet, activity, and behavioral support are the foundation for everyone, including people on medication and people who've had surgery. Pharmacotherapy is generally considered at a BMI of 30 or above, or 27 with a weight-related condition such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or obstructive sleep apnea. Bariatric surgery is generally considered at 40, or 35 with comorbidities.

These thresholds vary by country, and several are set lower for some Asian populations.

The main classes

GLP-1 receptor agonists

Semaglutide and liraglutide mimic a gut hormone called GLP-1, which slows stomach emptying and acts on appetite centers in the brain. In phase 3 trials, once-weekly semaglutide at the obesity dose produced mean weight loss of around 15 percent of body weight over 68 weeks, alongside lifestyle intervention.

Dual GIP and GLP-1 agonists

Tirzepatide acts on two receptors. Trials at the highest doses have reported mean weight loss in the region of 20 percent over 72 weeks.

Older and other options

Orlistat blocks absorption of some dietary fat, producing roughly 3 percent additional weight loss versus placebo, with gastrointestinal side effects that many people find unacceptable. Naltrexone-bupropion and phentermine-topiramate act on appetite pathways. Phentermine alone is approved for short-term use in some countries.

What the coverage usually leaves out

Side effects are common. With GLP-1 medications, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation affect a large minority, particularly while the dose is being increased. Stopping because of adverse effects is not rare.

Weight returns after stopping. In the STEP 1 extension study, participants regained about two thirds of the weight they'd lost within a year of withdrawal. These are treatments for a chronic condition, not a course you finish.

A meaningful share of the weight lost is lean tissue. In the STEP 1 body-composition substudy, lean mass accounted for a substantial part of the total lost, and that is broadly what happens with any rapid weight loss rather than something specific to these drugs. It is the reason protein intake and resistance training matter more on them, not less, and the reason very rapid loss is not automatically better.

There are real contraindications. GLP-1 agonists are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2, and are not for use in pregnancy.

Cost is a genuine barrier. Where these drugs aren't covered by insurance or state provision, the monthly price puts them out of reach for many people, and because stopping leads to regain, the cost is ongoing rather than one-time.

Compounded and grey-market versions carry additional risk. Regulators in several countries have issued warnings about dosing errors and adulterated products sold outside pharmacy channels.

What "15% of body weight" actually means for one person

Trial averages hide a wide spread. In STEP 1, mean loss on semaglutide was about 15%. Around 32% of participants reached 20% or more, while about 13% did not reach even 5%. If you start at 95 kg, the average outcome is around 14 kg. It is also entirely possible to be the person who loses 4 kg, and no one can tell you in advance which you will be.

Trial participants also received structured lifestyle support throughout, which is not what most people get when they collect a prescription. The published numbers describe the drug plus that support, not the drug alone.

What happens when you stop

This is the part that changes how you should think about starting. In the STEP 1 extension, participants who came off semaglutide regained about two thirds of the weight they had lost within a year, and cardiometabolic markers drifted back toward baseline alongside it.

That is not a failure of willpower. These medications work while they are being taken, in the same way blood pressure medication does. The practical question is therefore not "how much will I lose" but "what does the next several years look like", including whether you can afford it, tolerate it, and access it that long.

Cost and access, which the coverage usually skips

Where these drugs are not covered by insurance or state provision, the monthly list price puts them out of reach for many people, and because stopping leads to regain the cost is ongoing rather than a one-off course. Prices and coverage differ widely by country, so check what applies where you live rather than what you read in international coverage. Supply shortages have also interrupted treatment for people mid-course, which is its own risk given what withdrawal does.

This is a genuine reason someone might reasonably choose not to start, and it deserves to be part of the decision rather than a footnote.

Questions worth taking to an appointment

  • Given my history, am I a candidate for medication at all?
  • What effect is realistic for someone like me, and over what timeframe?
  • What side effects should I expect, and what would make us stop?
  • What happens if I need to come off it?
  • Is it covered here, and what does it cost if not?

What this site does

LoseWeight.net provides calculators and evidence summaries. We don't recommend medications, sell them, or link to pharmacies. If you find a site that offers to prescribe based on a questionnaire alone, treat that as a warning sign rather than convenience.

Whatever you and your doctor decide, the arithmetic underneath does not change. The planner works the same way on or off medication, and protein and resistance training matter more on these drugs, not less.
