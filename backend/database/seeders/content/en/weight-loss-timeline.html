Losing 15 kg takes most people somewhere between eight and twelve months at a sustainable rate, not the four or five months a simple calculator predicts. The difference isn't pessimism. It's that weight loss slows down as you go, and almost no online tool accounts for it.

Why the curve bends

Most timeline calculators divide the weight you want to lose by a fixed weekly rate. That draws a straight line and produces a date.

Real loss decelerates. Someone starting at 100 kg with a 500 calorie daily deficit loses noticeably faster in month one than in month nine, with identical adherence, because a lighter body burns less energy doing the same things. The intake that created a 500 calorie deficit at the start might create a 300 calorie deficit ten kilos later. Nothing has gone wrong. The arithmetic just changed underneath you.

What rate is realistic

Starting pointSustainable weekly rate
BMI 25 to 300.25 to 0.5 kg (0.5 to 1 lb)
BMI 30 to 350.5 to 0.75 kg (1 to 1.5 lb)
BMI 35 and above0.75 to 1 kg (1.5 to 2 lb)

A reasonable ceiling is 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight per week. Push past that and more of what you lose comes from muscle, and adherence tends to fall apart.

Ignore the first two weeks

Early loss is fast and mostly isn't fat. Cutting carbohydrate depletes glycogen in muscle and liver, and each gram of stored glycogen holds around 3 g of water. Losing 300 to 400 g of glycogen drops 1 to 2 kg on the scale within days.

That's real weight, and it's genuinely gone, but it doesn't repeat and it isn't a rate you can extrapolate from. Judge your actual speed from week three onward.

A worked example

Take a 35 year old woman, 168 cm, 88 kg, lightly active, aiming for 72 kg. Her resting rate is about 1,559 calories and her total daily expenditure about 2,144.

At a 20 percent deficit, around 1,715 calories a day, the straight-line estimate says 26 weeks. Recalculating her expenditure as she gets lighter puts it closer to 33 to 36 weeks. Both numbers come from correct arithmetic. Only one of them will match her scale.

That's roughly two extra months. Knowing about them in advance is the difference between a plan that survives month six and one that gets abandoned there.

Building your own

Calculate your expenditure at your current weight, pick a deficit you could hold during a bad week rather than a perfect one, and project forward while recalculating expenditure as the weight comes off. That last step is what produces the bend. Then assume something like 85 to 90 percent adherence, because that's what actually happens.

The part nobody plans for

The timeline that matters most isn't the one ending at your goal weight. It's the one continuing past it. Long-term follow-up of behavioral weight loss programs consistently shows partial regain when there's no explicit maintenance phase.

So decide now what your eating looks like at goal weight, not just on the way there. You'll spend far more of your life at maintenance than in a deficit, and it's the harder of the two to improvise.
