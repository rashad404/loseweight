If you're going to track one number for health rather than progress, make it your waist divided by your height. Keep it under 0.5. BMI is useful for comparing populations but blunt for any individual, and home body fat readings are too unreliable to act on.

What BMI is good for

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared. Under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 healthy, 25 to 29.9 overweight, 30 and above obesity.

It correlates with body fat across large groups and predicts population-level disease risk well, which is why epidemiology relies on it. It's also free and takes ten seconds.

The limitations are equally real. It can't distinguish muscle from fat, so a lifter and someone carrying visceral fat can share a BMI. It ignores where fat sits, which matters more than how much there is. And the cutoffs came largely from populations of European ancestry. Cardiometabolic risk rises at lower BMI in South Asian, Chinese, and several other Asian populations, and many guidelines use 23 rather than 25 as the overweight threshold there.

Waist to height ratio

Divide your waist circumference by your height, in the same units. Under 0.5 is the target, which is easy to remember as keeping your waist under half your height.

It tracks something BMI cannot: roughly how much fat sits around your organs rather than under your skin, and visceral fat carries more metabolic risk. In studies comparing the two, waist to height usually predicts cardiometabolic risk at least as well as BMI and often better, and it does so without separate cutoffs for different populations. It needs nothing but a tape measure.

That does not make it the better number for every purpose. BMI is what large studies and clinical thresholds are built on, so it is what your doctor will reach for and what treatment guidelines reference. Waist to height is the more useful thing to watch month to month. They answer different questions rather than one replacing the other.

Measure at the midpoint between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, at the end of a normal breath out, with the tape snug but not compressing the skin. Technique matters here: pulling the tape tight, measuring at the navel instead of the right landmark, or measuring after a large meal can each shift the number by a few centimeters. Measure the same way every time and compare against your own previous readings rather than against a table.

The ratio hasn't been validated for children under 6, during pregnancy, or in conditions that cause abdominal swelling.

Body fat percentage

Conceptually this is what you actually care about. The problem is measuring it.

DEXA is the practical reference standard, but it costs money and means a clinic visit. Bioimpedance scales are convenient and heavily affected by hydration, so the absolute number is unreliable, though a trend measured under consistent conditions has some value. Skinfold calipers are reasonable in trained hands and poor otherwise.

Unless you have consistent access to the same method, body fat percentage is more likely to mislead you than inform you.

What to actually track

Waist to height ratio monthly, as your main health marker. Weight as a 7 day average, daily, as your main progress marker. BMI once, to set a rough goal range. Body fat percentage only if you have reliable access to one method and want it.

Two people at the same BMI can have very different risk profiles, and the tape measure resolves more of that difference than the scale does.

Both numbers are worth knowing: BMI and the healthy weight range for your height, and waist to height, which is the one to re-measure monthly.
